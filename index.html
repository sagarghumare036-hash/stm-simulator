About this simulation

A scanning tunneling microscope (STM) images a conducting surface without a lens. An atomically sharp needle sits a few tenths of a nanometre above the sample, and the instrument measures the tiny current of electrons that tunnel across the vacuum gap even though they lack the energy to cross it classically.

How it works

  • The gap and the work function form a rectangular barrier for electrons.
  • Transmission falls off exponentially with gap width: T ≈ exp(−2κd), with κ ≈ 5.12·√φ per nm (φ in eV).
  • Current is proportional to T and to the bias voltage.
  • In constant-current mode the tip rises over atoms and dips between them to hold the current fixed, so its height traces the surface.
  • In constant-height mode the tip stays level and the current varies instead.

Controls

  • Tip height: the setpoint gap above the lattice (nm).
  • Work function: the barrier height (eV).
  • Bias voltage: the voltage across the junction.
  • Scan speed: how fast the tip rasters.

Did you know?

Moving the tip one ångström closer can change the current by roughly a factor of ten. Binnig and Rohrer shared the 1986 Nobel Prize in Physics for inventing the STM.
